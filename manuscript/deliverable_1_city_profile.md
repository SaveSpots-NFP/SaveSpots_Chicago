## Overdose burden

Cook County recorded two mortality products in 2024 that disagree by construction —
the same pattern the reference paper documents for Dallas, and worth stating
explicitly rather than picking one number and hiding the other. The **Cook County
Medical Examiner's Office** confirmed 1,026 opioid overdose deaths countywide for
calendar year 2024 (87% fentanyl-involved), the lowest count since 2015, as of its
January 2025 preliminary release. The **joint Cook County Department of Public
Health / Chicago Department of Public Health surveillance report**, released June
2026 with more complete case processing, instead finalizes county-wide 2024 opioid
deaths at 1,138 (835 Chicago, 279 suburban Cook County), down from 1,783 in 2023 and
1,961 in 2022 — a 42% decline from 2022 to 2024. Neither figure is wrong; they are
different products at different processing stages, exactly the distinction the
reference paper draws between VSRR provisional counts and DCHHS finalized counts.
This paper will cite each to its source and window and will not combine them,
following the research contract's classification standard (Measured/Modeled/
Published).

Using the finalized joint report: Chicago's 2024 opioid overdose mortality rate was
28.7 per 100,000 — falling 37.2% from 2023, but still nearly double the Illinois
state rate (14.6) and 1.6 times the national rate (17.9). Fentanyl was involved in
91.7% of Chicago's opioid deaths and 87.1% of suburban Cook County's. For 2025,
the Medical Examiner's Office has confirmed 683 opioid deaths (82% fentanyl-involved)
as of its most recent reporting — a provisional, partial-year figure that should be
read as a floor, not a settled value, exactly as the reference paper treats VSRR's
most recent months.

The city's drug supply is also multiply adulterated in ways that materially change
what "overdose response" means here. Xylazine ("tranq") was detected in 45 Illinois
overdose deaths in 2020 and over 200 in 2022, and unlike opioids, its effects are not
reversed by naloxone; it is also strongly associated with severe, non-fatal skin
wounds that bring users into contact with the health system through a route standard
overdose surveillance does not capture. More acutely, the CDC's May 2024
investigation into medetomidine-adulterated overdoses in Chicago — an emerging
veterinary sedative adulterant — identified 12 confirmed, 26 probable, and 140
suspected cases in a single week (May 11–17, 2024), the largest confirmed cluster to
date nationally. Ninety-two percent of confirmed cases showed only partial or no
improvement after naloxone administration, because medetomidine's effects are not
opioid-receptor-mediated. Affected individuals were 84% male, 87% non-Hispanic Black,
and 63% aged 45–64. On the single worst day of that cluster (May 11, 2024), EMS
responded to 54 suspected opioid overdoses in one day — double Chicago's 2023 daily
average — a non-fatal surveillance signal Dallas could not construct at all given
its data environment, and one this paper should treat as a primary measured quantity
candidate (see Deliverable 3).

## Demographics with implications

Chicago's population is approximately 2.71 million. Its overdose mortality burden is
racially stratified in a way that maps directly onto historical segregation patterns:
in 2024, Black Chicagoans experienced opioid overdose deaths at 2.5 times the rate of
white residents and nearly 4 times the rate of Hispanic residents, with similar
disparities in suburban Cook County. This is a materially different demographic
signature than Dallas, where overall mortality burden concentrated among White
individuals with the 2024 fentanyl shift moving toward Hispanic males — Chicago's
racial disparity is starker and, notably, has persisted despite a comparatively
well-resourced harm reduction infrastructure (see below), which is itself an
analytical finding: infrastructure existing is not the same as infrastructure
reaching the population bearing the highest burden.

Roughly 17% of Chicagoans live below the federal poverty line, with over 167,000
residents in "deep poverty" (household income below half the federal threshold).
Unlike Dallas County — where 21% of residents are uninsured because Texas has not
expanded Medicaid — Illinois expanded Medicaid under the ACA, and Chicago's uninsured
rate is approximately 9.75% (2024), less than half Dallas County's rate. This is a
first-order structural difference from the reference paper's data environment:
Illinois' Medicaid expansion produced an 83% drop in the uninsured rate among opioid-
related hospitalizations statewide, and 95% of Illinois Medicaid enrollees with
opioid use disorder are eligible specifically through the expansion population. The
implication for this paper is direct: insurance-driven treatment-access barriers,
which the reference paper identifies as Dallas's single largest structural barrier,
are substantially smaller in Chicago — meaning if Chicago's overdose burden remains
elevated relative to state and national rates despite lower uninsurance and more
permissive policy, the analytical story likely runs through access geography,
implementation reach, and supply adulteration rather than through coverage or law.

## Treatment and infectious disease

Illinois has 787 SAMHSA-verified treatment facilities offering detox, inpatient,
outpatient, and medication-assisted treatment, and the City of Chicago was named a
targeted geography under Illinois's Medicaid 1115 Demonstration Waiver for enhanced
substance use disorder services. Buprenorphine access has also been extended via
low-threshold, mobile-unit models — a 2023 published outcomes study documented an
academic-medical-center/community-harm-reduction-organization partnership delivering
buprenorphine via mobile medical unit directly into West Side overdose hot spots,
a model with no equivalent identified in the Dallas paper's implementation landscape.

Infectious disease burden among people who inject drugs (PWID) is substantial and
well-studied here relative to Dallas, where the equivalent evidence was a single jail
seroprevalence study. A meta-analysis of 20 years of Chicago-area PWID data estimates
approximately 32,000 PWID in metropolitan Chicago, with an aggregate HCV-RNA
(active infection) prevalence of 47% (~15,040 people). Critically, PWID who attend
harm reduction programs (syringe services) show a lower HCV-RNA prevalence (30%) than
the PWID population overall, and young PWID under 30 show still lower prevalence
(10%) — a pattern consistent with (though not proof of) a protective effect of harm
reduction program engagement, and a genuinely useful empirical anchor this paper can
use that Dallas's data environment did not offer. *(Flag: current HIV prevalence
among Chicago PWID was not confidently located in this pass — report as a gap unless
a primary IDPH/CDPH source is found in Deliverable 3.)*

## Criminal justice intersection

Illinois's legal environment is considerably more permissive than Texas's across
every axis the reference paper evaluates, and this needs to be stated plainly rather
than assumed. Illinois's Emergency Medical Services Access Law of 2012 (its Good
Samaritan law) provides actual immunity from prosecution for possession-related
charges for both the caller and the person overdosing, in contrast to the Jessica
Sosa Act's narrow affirmative defense in Texas. The Illinois Drug Overdose Prevention
Program Law (P.A. 096-0361, 2010) immunizes lay naloxone administration from civil or
criminal liability, and the state maintains a standing order permitting naloxone
dispensing without individual prescription. Illinois also authorizes syringe services
programs statewide (any governmental or nongovernmental entity may operate one), and
fentanyl test strips are explicitly excluded from the state's paraphernalia
definition and may be sold over the counter — the opposite legal status from Texas on
both counts.

Illinois is not without punitive drug law: possession of any Schedule I or II
narcotic (fentanyl is Schedule II) is a Class 4 felony (1–3 years, up to $25,000
fine) under 720 ILCS 570/402(c), and delivery of 1–14 grams of fentanyl is a Class 1
felony (4–15 years, up to $250,000). A 3-year sentencing enhancement applies
specifically to fentanyl-involved manufacture/delivery offenses — not to simple
possession — under 720 ILCS 570/401 (legislative proposals would raise this to 5
years for that tier). *(Corrected 2026-10-11 — see Deliverable 2 §4.6 for the full
citation-verification note; the prior version of this sentence overstated the
enhancement's reach.)* But Cook County has
implemented deflection programs that redirect people with substance use disorder to
community-based treatment as an alternative to arrest or the emergency room,
including law enforcement training on overdose/naloxone response, and the Cook
County Sheriff's Office runs a dedicated Treatment Response Team. This is a
structurally different posture than Dallas's DIVERT Court comparison — deflection
happens pre-arrest here, not post-charge — and is worth developing as a comparison
point in the Policy Matrix (Deliverable 2).

## Existing infrastructure

Chicago's harm reduction landscape is multi-actor and comparatively mature relative
to Dallas's fragmented, training-dependent model:

- **Chicago Recovery Alliance** distributes naloxone and sterile syringes via mobile
  clinics and a West Side warehouse, plus South and North Side outposts.
- **Community Outreach Intervention Projects (COIP)** operates storefront and
  van-based harm reduction on the west and south sides: syringes, naloxone, fentanyl
  test strips, point-of-care HIV/HCV testing, and treatment linkage.
- **West Side Heroin/Opioid Task Force**, convened 2016, relocated its office in
  October 2025 to Pulaski/Lake — the self-described "epicenter of the fentanyl
  crisis" in West Garfield Park — and runs street outreach, mobile MOUD vans, drug
  checking, and naloxone training/distribution.
- **Operation S.O.S.**, a CDPH/task-force door-knocking campaign, canvassed nearly
  1,300 West Side blocks that had at least one overdose-related EMS response,
  prioritizing blocks with 3+ responses in 2023, distributing naloxone and
  publicizing same-day MOUD access.
- **MAR NOW (Medication for Addiction Recovery, Now)** offers free, same-day
  buprenorphine/MOUD access, promoted through the door-knocking campaign.
- **CARE ORT (Crisis Assistance Response and Engagement Overdose Response Team)**,
  piloted February 2023: a paramedic + peer-recovery-coach team that proactively
  follows up with overdose survivors post-EMS-encounter to offer treatment linkage
  and harm reduction supplies — a "high-leverage window" intervention the reference
  paper explicitly identifies as absent in Dallas.
- **Cook County Health** provides free naloxone at health centers and at Stroger and
  Provident Hospital emergency departments.
- A published mobile buprenorphine unit (academic medical center + community harm
  reduction organization partnership) targets West Side overdose hot spots directly.

The West Side concentration is measured, not assumed: Austin, East Garfield Park,
West Garfield Park, Humboldt Park, and North Lawndale together accounted for 4,155 of
11,419 citywide EMS overdose-response incidents (36%) in the period examined by one
cited analysis, with West Garfield Park (ZIP 60624), South Austin (60644), and East
Garfield Park (60612) as the three highest-death ZIP codes.

## Who is missed

Three populations stand out as underserved by an infrastructure that is, on paper,
more extensive than Dallas's:

1. **Older Black men on the West Side.** The medetomidine cluster's demographic
   signature (84% male, 87% Black, 63% aged 45–64) and the citywide finding that
   Black Chicagoans die at 2.5–4x the rate of other groups together indicate that
   existing outreach — built substantially around younger, opioid-only overdose
   response — is not reaching this specific population proportionally to its risk,
   despite Chicago's comparatively dense harm reduction footprint.
2. **People affected by naloxone-resistant adulterants.** Xylazine and medetomidine
   both blunt or defeat naloxone's effect. A harm reduction infrastructure built
   principally around naloxone distribution — which describes nearly every program
   listed above — is structurally mismatched to a supply that is increasingly not
   opioid-only. Wound care (from xylazine) and non-opioid sedation (from
   medetomidine) are the presenting problems for a growing share of this population,
   not "overdose" in the classic sense outreach is designed to intercept.
3. **Hispanic/Latino residents in neighborhoods like Pilsen and Little Village.** No
   language-specific harm reduction program or bilingual-capacity data was identified
   in this pass, paralleling the reference paper's finding for Dallas (a documented
   absence rather than a confirmed presence or absence — needs direct outreach-org
   contact or a records request in Deliverable 3, not an assumption either way).

## The single most critical gap

Chicago's problem is not, primarily, that harm reduction infrastructure or
permissive policy is absent — Illinois has both a real Good Samaritan law and
authorized syringe services, and Chicago has a dense, multi-organization outreach
network Dallas does not. The critical gap instead is a **mismatch between what the
existing infrastructure is built to treat (opioid-only overdose, reversible by
naloxone) and both who is dying and what is increasingly in the drug supply**: older
Black men on the West Side die at multiples of the citywide rate despite the
infrastructure's geographic concentration in that same area, and a rising share of
local overdoses involve xylazine or medetomidine, adulterants naloxone does not
reverse. This reframes the paper's central question away from Dallas's "how do we
get naloxone to more people" toward "why does naloxone-centered infrastructure that
already covers this geography and population on paper still fail to close the
mortality disparity, and what does the local supply's chemistry mean for what
'harm reduction access' should even measure here."
