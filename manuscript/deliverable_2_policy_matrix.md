## 4.1 Naloxone Access Laws

Illinois naloxone access runs through three stacked layers. First, a 2015 amendment
to the Illinois Pharmacy Practice Act (225 ILCS 85/19.1) established a statewide
naloxone standing order, letting pharmacies dispense without an individual
prescription — functionally similar to Texas's TXOTI standing-order framework.
Second, the Illinois Drug Overdose Prevention Program Law (Public Act 096-0361,
2010), strengthened by Public Act 099-0480 (2015), immunizes any lay person —
family, friends, bystanders — from civil or criminal liability for administering
naloxone. Third, IDPH/IDFPR maintain a standardized procedure (under the Substance
Use Disorder Act, 20 ILCS 301/) letting trained non-clinical staff obtain, dispense,
or administer naloxone and nalmefene. Federally, 2023's OTC naloxone approval applies
here identically to Dallas, with the same $40–50 retail-price uptake ceiling the
reference paper documents nationally.

**What it does in practice:** this three-layer structure is the legal foundation for
every naloxone-distributing organization identified in Deliverable 1 — Chicago
Recovery Alliance, COIP, the West Side Heroin/Opioid Task Force, Cook County Health —
none of which required a special statutory carve-out to operate. This is a materially
more permissive starting position than Dallas's single standing-order mechanism.
**Strategic implication:** the naloxone-access legal environment is not the binding
constraint on Chicago's outcomes; see Deliverable 1's central finding that the gap is
implementation-reach and supply-chemistry, not legal access.

## 4.2 Good Samaritan Law

Illinois's Emergency Medical Services Access Law (720 ILCS 570/414, added by Public
Act 097-0678) provides **actual immunity from arrest, charge, or prosecution** — not
merely an affirmative defense, the structure Texas's Jessica Sosa Act uses — for a
person who in good faith seeks or obtains emergency help for an overdose, covering
both the caller and the person overdosing. Immunized offenses include Illinois
Controlled Substances Act possession (§§401–402), drug paraphernalia possession
(Drug Paraphernalia Control Act §3.5), and Methamphetamine Control Act violations
(§§55–60). No repeat-use bar comparable to the Jessica Sosa Act's 18-month limit
was identified in this pass.

Immunity is not unconditional: it is capped by substance-specific quantity
thresholds (under 3g for heroin/cocaine/morphine/LSD, under 40g for peyote/
barbiturates/amphetamines, under 6g for pentazocine/methaqualone/PCP/ketamine) and is
forfeited if law enforcement already had independent reasonable suspicion or probable
cause before the call was made. Fentanyl is not named explicitly; it falls under a
catchall "other Schedule I/II narcotic" threshold of under 40g — a quantity that, given
fentanyl's potency (effective doses are measured in milligrams), covers the
overwhelming majority of realistic personal-possession scenarios. In this respect the
threshold structure, though seemingly generic, functions more protectively for
fentanyl-era overdoses than it might first appear.

**What it does in practice:** a substantively stronger deterrent-reduction tool than
Dallas has, on paper. **Strategic implication:** rather than a legislative-fix
recommendation (Dallas's top ask), this paper's equivalent recommendation is likely
an awareness/education gap — GAO's finding that protection only reduces death rates
where bystanders *know* the protection exists applies regardless of how strong the
underlying statute is, and this paper should investigate whether West Side residents
are aware of 720 ILCS 570/414 before assuming the law's strength translates into
practice.

## 4.3 Fentanyl Test Strip Legality

Illinois explicitly excludes "equipment, products, or materials to analyze or test
for the presence of fentanyl, a fentanyl analogue, or a drug adulterant" from its
statutory definition of drug paraphernalia. Pharmacists and retailers may sell test
strips over the counter; county health departments may distribute them free; and
syringe services programs are required to provide access to them "if feasible."
This is the direct opposite legal status from Texas, where test strips remain a
Class C misdemeanor under paraphernalia law despite unanimous 2025 House support for
legalization.

**What it does in practice:** removes the single policy barrier the reference paper
identifies as most immediately fixable in a restrictive state — Illinois has already
made this fix. **Strategic implication:** for Chicago, drug-checking capacity is a
distribution and awareness question, not a legal one — worth investigating whether
test strips reach the West Side at a rate proportional to need, and whether their
availability has kept pace with the xylazine/medetomidine adulteration Deliverable 1
documents (test strips detect fentanyl, not xylazine or medetomidine — a real
capability gap independent of legality).

## 4.4 Syringe Services Programs

Any governmental or nongovernmental organization — including a local health
department, community-based organization, or other entity promoting harm reduction —
may establish and operate a needle and syringe access program under Illinois law.
This is a full statewide authorization, contrasted with Texas's single-county Bexar
pilot with pending (Dallas-excluding) expansion legislation.

**What it does in practice:** legally clears the path for COIP's storefront/van
syringe programs and any future West Side expansion without requiring special
authorization. **Strategic implication:** because authorization is not the
constraint here, any remaining syringe-access gap in Chicago is a resourcing,
siting, or awareness gap — a genuinely different diagnostic question than Dallas's
"is this even legal here."

## 4.5 Medication-Assisted Treatment (MAT) Environment

Methadone remains dispensable only through federally certified opioid treatment
programs (OTPs) — a federal constraint identical in every state, including Illinois
and Texas. A 2024 federal rule change (applicable nationwide) loosened this somewhat:
stable patients may now receive up to 28 days of take-home methadone, and initial
admission no longer requires a documented one-year prior history of opioid use —
both relevant, recent loosenings this paper should note apply here just as they would
in any 2026 analysis, including a hypothetical Dallas update.

Buprenorphine prescribing was liberalized nationally by the 2023 elimination of the
DATA 2000 waiver requirement, identical to Dallas's finding. However, research on
Illinois-specific waivered practitioners found many under-prescribed relative to
their patient limits or had not prescribed buprenorphine at all in the preceding
year — meaning capacity exists on paper more than it is used in practice, an
implementation gap distinct from a legal one. Cook County Health's Bridge Clinic
offers rapid-access, low-threshold buprenorphine with no-judgment intake, and has
integrated methadone-adjacent care into the Austin Health Center on the West Side —
a concrete, named example of embedded MAT access in the exact geography Deliverable 1
identifies as highest-burden, something Dallas's analysis did not have an equivalent
of.

**Strategic implication:** Illinois's MAT environment is federally constrained in the
same ways as Texas's, but is meaningfully better resourced at the point of delivery
(Bridge Clinic, mobile buprenorphine units) — the gap here is prescriber utilization
of existing legal capacity, not the capacity itself.

## 4.6 Drug Possession and Penalty Structure

Possession of any amount of a Schedule I or II narcotic (fentanyl is Schedule II) is
a Class 4 felony (1–3 years, up to $25,000 fine) — with a statutory "410 probation"
option allowing first-time offenders to plead guilty without entering judgment, on
consent. Delivery of 1–14 grams of fentanyl is a Class 1 felony (4–15 years, up to
$250,000). A sentencing enhancement adds 3 years to any drug offense involving any
amount of fentanyl (legislative proposals as of this pass would raise this to 5
years). This is a meaningfully lower penalty ceiling than Texas's Penalty Group 1-B
structure, which scales up to 99 years or life imprisonment for large fentanyl
quantities and permits murder charges for a fatal fentanyl-related sale.

Cook County additionally operates pre-arrest deflection programs — redirecting
people with substance use disorder to community-based treatment before arrest, with
associated law-enforcement training on overdose response — plus a dedicated
Sheriff's Office Treatment Response Team and both felony and misdemeanor diversion
programs through the Cook County State's Attorney. This is a structurally different
intervention point than Dallas's DIVERT Court comparison (post-charge, voluntary
diversion for first-time felony defendants): Cook County's deflection model
intervenes *before* arrest, not after charging.

**Strategic implication:** possession-related legal exposure is a smaller deterrent
to emergency-calling and outreach engagement here than in Texas, both because
penalties are lower-ceiling and because deflection intervenes earlier in the
process — worth testing whether this translates into higher self-reported comfort
calling 911 among West Side residents (a qualitative question for Section 5/7, not
resolvable from policy text alone).

## 4.7 Medicaid Expansion and Insurance

Illinois expanded Medicaid under the ACA; Texas did not. This is the single largest
structural divergence between the two states' harm reduction environments. Chicago's
uninsured rate is approximately 9.75% (2024) versus Dallas County's 21%. Expansion
produced an 83% drop in the uninsured rate specifically among opioid-related
hospitalizations statewide in Illinois, and 95% of Illinois Medicaid enrollees with
opioid use disorder are eligible through the expansion population — meaning
Medicaid expansion is not a peripheral benefit here but the primary coverage pathway
for the population this paper is about. Chicago was additionally named a targeted
geography under Illinois's Medicaid 1115 Demonstration Waiver for enhanced substance
use disorder services.

**Strategic implication:** every barrier the reference paper attributes to Texas's
Medicaid non-expansion (KFF's cited 30% lower treatment-uptake likelihood, 6% higher
opioid death rate in non-expansion states) should be read as *not* independently
operating in Chicago — reinforcing Deliverable 1's framing that this paper's central
tension is not coverage but reach and supply chemistry.

## 4.8 Opioid Settlement Fund Distribution

Illinois's settlement allocation structure differs from Texas's: 55% to the state
Opioid Remediation Fund, 20% to the state directly, and 25% to local governments
(counties and eligible municipalities) — versus Texas's 70/15/15 split favoring the
state abatement fund more heavily. As of September 2025, the Remediation Fund had
received $292 million cumulative, with total settlements-to-date projected near $795
million through 2038 and a current annual allocation pace of roughly $44 million/year.
Reported 2024 Remediation Fund line items include warm hand-off/recovery services
($33M), medication-assisted treatment ($21.8M), and prevention programming ($24.6M)
— though whether these figures represent a single fiscal year or a cumulative total
needs direct confirmation from the IDHS annual report before use in the manuscript.

A notable data gap: unlike Texas, where the Baker Institute's analysis of 21
localities' settlement spending was possible because of accessible local reporting,
**Illinois's 25% local-government share is not centrally or publicly published** —
counties and the City of Chicago must report to the state, but there is no
requirement that those reports be made publicly accessible. This means Chicago- and
Cook-County-specific settlement spending (as opposed to the statewide Remediation
Fund) may not be independently verifiable from open data — a genuine limitation this
paper should document explicitly, mirroring the reference paper's practice of
reporting gaps as gaps rather than estimating around them.

**Strategic implication:** the state-level funding pool is real and comparatively
well-documented, but this paper likely cannot produce a Chicago/Cook-County-specific
settlement-spending breakdown analogous to Dallas's "$7 million to forensic
toxicology" finding without a records request — flag for Deliverable 3.
